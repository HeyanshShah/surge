    What you are looking at

    On the night of August 26, 2017, Houston's 911 system took more than 56,000 calls in 17 hours, against a normal 8,000 a day. Callers waited on hold, hung up and called again, and many of the calls were about people already rescued or people a dispatcher could not reach. Surge replays that night on the real city and asks what a dispatcher with a copilot could have done differently.

    Hear

    Each call's words become a structured incident: what is happening, the exact street address (spoken digit by digit, misspelled or abbreviated), who is inside, and how deep the water is. Every conclusion points back to the words that produced it.

    Merge

    Repeat callers, neighbors and relatives across town reporting the same family become one incident instead of five, using phone number, address and location fix.

    Forecast

    A terrain flood model (height above nearest drainage, from USGS-derived elevation and the real bayou network) predicts when the water at each address reaches waist and chest height, and which roads close to each vehicle.

    Assign

    Every few minutes the copilot solves an optimal assignment of free units to waiting incidents, weighing lives saved now against later on actual drive times over the flooded road network.

    Live mode

    The same engine runs on today's water. Live mode reads every USGS stream gauge in the city (updated every 5 to 15 minutes) and the National Weather Service's official river forecasts, and drives the terrain model one bayou at a time: each channel follows the nearest gauge on its own stream. Slide the outlook forward to see which roads close to cars and trucks, which addresses go under, which stations get cut off, and where water reaches chest height first so boats can be staged there before the calls start. Log a real call and the copilot recommends a unit over the roads as they are. surge serve runs the same console for a whole dispatch floor with a JSON API for call intake, and data/fetch.sh builds any US city from open data.

    Benchmark

    Same storm, same calls, same call-takers and same 137 units for every dispatcher. Averaged over 20 storms the copilot never saw while it was being tuned.

    Data

      The storm timing, the calls and their wording are simulated; the city is real. The risk model is deliberately simple and is the same model the copilot uses to plan, so read the benchmark as a comparison of dispatch strategies, not a forecast of deaths. This is a research prototype, not a medical or emergency tool.
